A single rotation covers the simple case. Real coverage often needs more: a daytime team that hands off to an after-hours rotation, or a roster that follows working hours around the globe. You build both by **stacking layers** and **restricting** each one to the hours it should cover.

:::note[Availability]
On-Call Schedules are a paid Incident Management feature. If you don't see **On-Call Schedules** in the Incident Management navigation, your plan doesn't include it yet — contact your account team to turn it on.
:::

## How layer priority works

Layers stack top to bottom. At any moment, the **highest layer with an active participant wins**. A layer further down only takes over where the layer above it is restricted out or has no one rostered.

That's the whole trick behind tiered coverage: put the narrow, high-priority rotation on top and a broad fallback underneath. Where the top layer is "off" — outside its restriction — the moment falls through to the next layer.

The full resolution order (overrides first, then layers top to bottom, then nobody) is in the [overview](../). Keep it in mind here: restrictions are what create the gaps that lower layers fill.

## Restriction types

Each layer carries one restriction setting:

- **None** — the layer is always active. This is the default, and the right choice for a 24/7 fallback layer.
- **Time of day** — the same daily window, every day. For example, 09:00–17:00.
- **Day of week** — a window per weekday, so weekdays and weekends can differ.

Windows are expressed in the schedule's timezone as a start and end time. A window may **cross midnight**: a 22:00–06:00 window covers the late evening and the early hours of the next day.

## Example: business-hours primary with a 24/7 backup

Two layers give you a daytime team that hands off to an on-call rotation every night and weekend.

| | Layer 1 (top) | Layer 2 (bottom) |
|---|---|---|
| Participants | Day-shift team | On-call engineers |
| Rotation | Weekly | Weekly |
| Restriction | Day of week, Mon–Fri 09:00–17:00 | None |

During business hours, Layer 1 is active and holds the pager. Outside that window — nights and weekends — Layer 1 is restricted out, so the moment falls through to Layer 2. The backup layer carries no restriction, so it's always there to catch what the primary doesn't cover.

## Example: follow-the-sun

To hand coverage around the globe, give each region its own layer restricted to its working hours. Because every window is evaluated in the **schedule's** timezone, pick one timezone for the schedule and express each region's hours in it. Using UTC keeps the math obvious:

| Layer | Region | Restriction (time of day, UTC) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | APAC | 00:00–08:00 |
| 2 | EMEA | 08:00–16:00 |
| 3 | Americas | 16:00–24:00 |

Each layer has its own participants and rotation. As the clock advances, exactly one layer is active at a time, so the pager rotates around the world without anyone covering the middle of their night.

:::caution[Restriction windows use the schedule's timezone]
A window of 09:00–17:00 means 09:00–17:00 in the schedule's timezone — not in each participant's local time. Set the schedule's timezone deliberately, and translate each region's hours into it. This is the most common follow-the-sun mistake.
:::

## Avoid leaving no one on call

Restrictions create gaps on purpose, but a stack made entirely of restricted layers can leave moments with **nobody on call**. If an incident fires during such a gap, an escalation step that pages the schedule has no one to reach.

The fix is a habit: any schedule an escalation policy depends on should end with a **no-restriction layer at the bottom** as a catch-all. Use the preview to scan for uncovered windows before you rely on the schedule.
